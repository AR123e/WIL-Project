## Chess Tactics and Common Terms (project-authored glossary)

Source: Written for this project as a plain-language glossary of standard chess terms. It is original text, not a quotation from any publication.

## Glossary: tactics and common terms

tactic: a short, forcing sequence of moves that wins material or gives checkmate, such as a fork, pin or skewer. A tactic is a short-term idea, while a strategy is a long-term plan.

strategy: the long-term plan for a game, such as controlling the centre, improving piece positions or creating a weakness in the opponent's position.

fork: a single piece attacks two or more enemy pieces at the same time, so the opponent can usually save only one of them. Knights are famous for forks, but queens and pawns can fork too.

pin: a piece is pinned when it cannot or should not move because a more valuable piece stands behind it on the same line. In an absolute pin the piece shields the king, so moving it would be illegal. In a relative pin moving it is legal but would lose the more valuable piece.

skewer: an attack on a valuable piece that must move away, which exposes a less valuable piece behind it to capture. A skewer is like a pin in reverse.

discovered attack: moving one piece out of the way so that another piece behind it attacks something. If the revealed attack is on the enemy king, it is called a discovered check.

double check: two pieces give check at the same time. The only way to answer a double check is to move the king.

zugzwang: a position in which the player to move would like to pass but cannot, because every legal move makes their position worse. The word is German for compulsion to move, and it is most common in endgames.

gambit: an opening in which a player sacrifices a pawn, or sometimes more, to gain faster development or an attack.

sacrifice: to give up material on purpose in return for an attack, a better position or checkmate.

tempo: a unit of time in chess equal to one move. Gaining a tempo means making progress while forcing the opponent to waste a move, and losing a tempo means wasting a move.

development: moving your pieces from their starting squares to active squares where they can control the board.

fianchetto: developing a bishop to b2, g2, b7 or g7, usually after moving the knight pawn one square, so the bishop controls the long diagonal.

opposition: a king-and-pawn endgame idea in which the two kings face each other with one empty square between them. The player who has to move must step aside, which can let the other king advance.

passed pawn: a pawn with no enemy pawns in front of it on its own file or the files next to it, so no enemy pawn can block or capture it as it advances. Passed pawns are very dangerous in the endgame.

doubled pawns: two pawns of the same colour on the same file, which is usually a weakness.

isolated pawn: a pawn with no friendly pawns on the files next to it, so other pawns cannot defend it.

open file: a file with no pawns on it. Rooks are strong on open files.

back rank mate: a checkmate on a king that is trapped on its first rank by its own pawns, usually delivered by a rook or queen.

scholar's mate: a very quick checkmate in as few as four moves in which the queen and bishop attack the weak f7 square, or f2 for Black. Beginners can avoid it by defending that square.

fool's mate: the quickest possible checkmate, in only two moves, which happens only if White plays very weak moves.

exchange: trading one piece for another. Winning the exchange means winning a rook, worth 5 points, for a knight or bishop, worth 3 points.

blunder: a serious mistake that loses material or the game.

hanging piece: a piece that is attacked and not defended, so it can be captured for free.

material: the pieces and pawns each side has. Being ahead in material means having more total point value.

initiative: the ability to keep making threats so that the opponent must keep responding.

major pieces: the queens and rooks. The bishops and knights are called minor pieces.

bishop pair: having both bishops while the opponent does not. Two bishops working together are often an advantage in open positions.

kingside castling: castling with the rook on the h-file, also called short castling and written O-O.

queenside castling: castling with the rook on the a-file, also called long castling and written O-O-O.
